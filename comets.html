Module 02

Comets: Icy Wanderers

Frozen relics from the outer solar system that blaze into glowing spectacles whenever they swing near the Sun.

What They're Made Of

Dirty Snowballs From the Deep Freeze

A comet's solid core — its nucleus — is often described as a "dirty snowball," though scientists now prefer "icy dirtball," since it's mostly rock and dust held together by ices.

That nucleus is a mix of water ice, frozen carbon dioxide (dry ice), ammonia, methane, and dust — material that has stayed frozen since the solar system formed, because it condensed far from the Sun's heat.

Most comet nuclei are surprisingly small, typically just 1 to 20 km across — yet they can grow comas and tails hundreds of thousands of kilometers long.

A comet nucleus: ice and rock, frozen since the solar system's birth.

Coma

As a comet nears the Sun, heat causes its ices to sublimate — turning directly from solid to gas. This releases gas and dust that form a glowing cloud around the nucleus called the coma, which can grow larger than a planet.

Two Tails, Always Pointing Away From the Sun

Solar wind and radiation pressure blow that gas and dust away from the nucleus, forming two distinct tails: a dust tail (yellowish, curved, reflecting sunlight) and an ion tail (blue, straight, made of charged gas pushed by solar wind). Both tails always point away from the Sun — not behind the direction of travel — which is why a comet's tail can actually lead the way as it leaves the Sun's neighborhood.

Why They Have Tails

Built by Sunlight and Solar Wind

Far from the Sun, a comet is just a dark, frozen nucleus — no coma, no tail, nearly invisible. It's only the Sun's heat and particles that transform it into the dramatic object we recognize.

The closer a comet gets to the Sun, the more intense the sublimation, and the bigger and brighter its coma and tails become. Try it yourself below.

Both tails stream away from the Sun — the ion tail straight, the heavier dust tail curving behind the comet's path.

Orbits

Long, Stretched-Out Journeys

Comets travel in highly elliptical (elongated) orbits that swing them from the freezing outer solar system to a close, fiery pass by the Sun, and back out again.

Short-period comets (orbits under 200 years) usually come from the Kuiper Belt, a disk of icy bodies beyond Neptune. Long-period comets, with orbits lasting thousands of years, are thought to come from the Oort Cloud — a vast, distant shell of icy objects surrounding the entire solar system.

Famous Comet

Halley's Comet

The most famous comet in history, Halley's Comet returns to the inner solar system roughly every 76 years. It's named after astronomer Edmond Halley, who in 1705 used Newton's laws to correctly predict its return in 1758 — proving comets follow the same physics as planets.

Halley last appeared in 1986, when it was visited by several spacecraft, and it's due to return in 2061. It's the only known short-period comet reliably visible to the naked eye from Earth, twice in a human lifetime.
