Module 03

Near-Earth Asteroids: Guarding Our Planet

Thousands of asteroids share Earth's neighborhood. Here's how scientists find them, track them, and — if needed — push them out of the way.

What They Are

Objects That Share Our Orbital Neighborhood

A Near-Earth Object (NEO) is any asteroid or comet whose orbit brings it within 1.3 astronomical units of the Sun — close enough that it can cross, or come near, Earth's own orbit.

Most NEOs are near-Earth asteroids (NEAs) that wandered in from the main asteroid belt after a gravitational nudge from Jupiter or a collision. The overwhelming majority pass by harmlessly — but a small fraction are classified as potentially hazardous asteroids (PHAs): larger than about 140 meters and capable of passing within 7.5 million km (0.05 AU) of Earth's orbit.

A NEO's stretched orbit can cross Earth's — which is why we track them decades ahead.

Tracking

How Scientists Track Asteroids

A global network of telescopes and radar dishes scans the sky every clear night, hunting for faint, fast-moving points of light.

Ground-Based Surveys

Automated survey telescopes like the Catalina Sky Survey, Pan-STARRS, and ATLAS scan huge swaths of sky nightly, comparing images to spot objects that move against the background stars.

Space-Based Telescopes

Missions like NEOWISE detect asteroids in infrared light — even dark ones that reflect very little visible sunlight — and help measure their true size.

Planetary Radar

Radar dishes such as Goldstone bounce radio waves off passing asteroids, revealing their precise shape, spin, surface features, and orbit with remarkable accuracy.

Every detection is reported to the Minor Planet Center, which combines global observations to calculate — and continuously refine — each object's orbit decades into the future.

Protecting Earth

Measuring the Risk

When a newly discovered asteroid's orbit is uncertain, NASA's Center for Near-Earth Object Studies (CNEOS) and ESA's planetary defense teams calculate the odds of a future impact and communicate the risk using the Torino Scale — a simple 0-to-10 scale running from “no hazard” all the way up to “certain collision.”

As more observations come in, orbits get refined and almost every object's risk rating drops to zero. Nearly all NEOs discovered so far have been ruled out as impact threats for the next century.

If a Threat Were Ever Found

Unlike in the movies, planetary defense isn't about blowing an asteroid up — it's about nudging it, years or decades in advance, just enough to miss Earth entirely.

Kinetic Impactor

Slam a spacecraft directly into the asteroid at high speed to change its velocity and orbit — proven for the first time by NASA's DART mission.

Gravity Tractor

Fly a spacecraft alongside the asteroid for years, using its tiny gravitational pull to slowly tug the asteroid onto a different path.

Nuclear Deflection

A last-resort option for very large or late-detected asteroids — a nearby nuclear detonation would vaporize surface material to push the asteroid off course without shattering it.

Mission Spotlight

DART: Proving We Can Do It

On September 26, 2022, NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) spacecraft deliberately slammed into Dimorphos, a small moonlet orbiting the larger asteroid Didymos — neither of which posed any threat to Earth, making it a safe real-world test target.

Traveling at about 6.6 km/s, the collision shortened Dimorphos's orbit around Didymos by roughly 32-33 minutes — far beyond the mission's minimum success threshold of just 73 seconds. It was humanity's first demonstration that we can actually change the path of an asteroid.
