Asteroids: Leftovers of Creation

Rocky and metallic worlds too small to become planets, drifting through the solar system since its very beginning.

What They Are

Rubble From an Unfinished Planet

Asteroids are rocky, metallic bodies left over from the formation of the solar system roughly 4.6 billion years ago. They're too small and too irregular to be considered planets — most are just a few meters to a few hundred kilometers across.

Unlike comets, asteroids formed closer to the Sun, in a region too warm for ice to survive, so they're made mostly of rock and metal rather than ice.

The Solar Nebula

About 4.6 billion years ago, a giant cloud of gas and dust — the solar nebula — collapsed under its own gravity. Most of the material fell into the center to form the Sun, while the rest flattened into a spinning disk.

Within that disk, dust grains stuck together, forming pebbles, then boulders, then planetesimals — the building blocks of planets.

Where They Come From

Planet-Building Material That Never Finished the Job

Most planetesimals eventually merged into the planets we know today. But in one region — between Mars and Jupiter — something interrupted the process.

Jupiter's immense gravity stirred up that neighborhood so much that the planetesimals there kept colliding and breaking apart instead of combining into a planet. The result: a vast field of leftover rubble, still orbiting the Sun today.

The Neighborhood

The Asteroid Belt

Most asteroids live in the main asteroid belt, a donut-shaped region between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, roughly 2.2 to 3.2 astronomical units (AU) from the Sun.

Despite what movies show, the belt is mostly empty space — spacecraft have flown through it many times without incident. If you gathered every asteroid in the belt into one ball, it would be smaller than Earth's Moon.

The largest object in the belt, Ceres, is so big and round that it's classified as a dwarf planet.

Composition

What Asteroids Are Made Of

Asteroids fall into three broad compositional families, based on how sunlight reflects off their surfaces.

C-type (Carbonaceous)

The most common type — about 75% of known asteroids. Dark, carbon-rich, and largely unchanged since the solar system formed. Bennu is a C-type asteroid.

S-type (Silicaceous)

About 17% of asteroids. Made of silicate rock and nickel-iron metal. Brighter than C-types and common in the inner asteroid belt.

M-type (Metallic)

A smaller group made mostly of nickel-iron metal, likely fragments of the cores of larger bodies that broke apart in ancient collisions.

Famous Asteroids

Meet the Neighbors

Bennu

A small, carbon-rich, near-Earth asteroid about 490 meters across — roughly the height of the Empire State Building. NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission visited Bennu, collected a sample of its surface, and returned it to Earth in 2023. Bennu is also tracked as a potentially hazardous asteroid, with a very small chance of a distant future close approach.

Vesta

The second most massive object in the asteroid belt, about 525 km across. Unlike most asteroids, Vesta is differentiated — it has a metallic core, rocky mantle, and crust, much like a small planet. NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited Vesta in 2011–2012, revealing giant impact craters and towering cliffs.
